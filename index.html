Founder

From 10K to 100K — the plan that worked for me

I used to practise Karate in my childhood, but after a gap of many years I started again from scratch — with beginner workouts and running. I began with a single 10K. Three years later I was finishing ultras. What changed wasn't talent — it was a patient, repeatable system that built my endurance and my strength. This is it.

DV

My first 10K felt like the edge of what I could do. So I stopped chasing speed and started chasing distance instead — slowly, one long run at a time, week after week for three years.

Along the way I ran a 115 km stadium run, a 100 km trail through the forests and hills of Mahabaleshwar, and 50K more times than I can count. Nothing about it was clever. I ran a lot of easy kilometres, I ran hills and trails, I lifted weights minimum 4 days a week along with CrossFit workouts and karate exercises, and I never stopped showing up.

The plans on this page grew out of that journey. It built my endurance and my strength — and it can build yours.

— Founder, Dhavakveer

What built my endurance and strength

Seven things did almost all of the work. None of them are complicated — the hard part is doing them for years.

Volume before speed

The base
  • Most of my running was easy and conversational. Distance came from many gentle kilometres, not many hard ones.

The long run is king

Every week
  • One long, slow run a week. That single session built the endurance everything else stood on.

Hills & trails

Strength
  • The climbs of Mahabaleshwar did more for my legs than any machine. Hills and uneven trail built strength and resilience.

Back-to-back long runs

Ultra prep
  • Long on Saturday, long again on Sunday. It taught my body to keep going on tired legs — exactly what 50K and 100K demand.

Strength training

Four days a week
  • Gym work at least four days a week, alongside CrossFit and karate exercises — squats, single-leg work and core kept me injury-free through huge mileage.

Consistency over intensity

The secret
  • I built over years, not weeks. Showing up week after week is the whole trick.

Bodyweight & yoga

When not in the gym
  • On non-gym days I still do 100 push-ups, 100 squats and 100 ab exercises — varying the movements so it never gets stale.
  • Yoga twice a week — Surya Namaskar, pranayam and other asanas — plus stretching on rest days.

My signature sessions

The handful of workouts that mattered most when the distances got serious.

The stadium long run

2–4 hours
  • Hours of laps on a flat track. No scenery, no shortcuts — pure mental toughness and steady endurance. This is what carried me through the 111 km stadium run.

The SRT long run

Singhgad–Rajgad–Torna
  • Genuinely tough. The last 10–12 km from Torna is a stiff climb that arrives right at the end — and drains whatever energy you have left.

The Mahabaleshwar trail long run

4–6 hours
  • Forest and hills: walk the steep climbs, run the flats and descents, eat and drink on the move. Time on feet for the 100 km.

The back-to-back weekend

Sat + Sun
  • A long Saturday followed by a long Sunday. The single best way I found to prepare the body for ultra distance.

The easy base run

60–90 min
  • A pace where I could hold a conversation the whole way. Most of my kilometres lived here — and the reason I never burned out.

02

Sprint training & workout plans

Sprinting is a power sport, not a distance one — training is about quality over quantity: short maximal efforts with full recovery, plus strength and plyometric work. Choose your event and level. Sessions are written as sets × reps — always sprint at the prescribed intensity, and stop a set the moment your form breaks down.

Sprint training essentials

Every session
  • Warm up thoroughly — 15–20 min easy jog, drills and skips, then 3–4 progressive strides. Never sprint cold.
  • Full recovery between reps — 3–8 min walking rest so every rep is truly maximal. If you're still breathing hard, you're not sprinting.
  • Quality beats fatigue. Sprint work is not conditioning — end a set the moment speed drops or technique breaks down.
  • High / low rhythm — separate hard speed days with easy tempo, mobility or rest so the nervous system recovers.

Before & after a sprint session

Competition day
  • Pre-meet: arrive early, complete a full warm-up, rehearse your block start and check the track and spikes.
  • Between rounds: stay warm in a tracksuit, sip fluids, and do a short re-warm-up before the next round.
  • Post-session: cool down with an easy jog, stretch, refuel with protein and carbs, and prioritise sleep.
  • Recovery: allow 48 hours between maximal speed sessions. Sore hamstrings or Achilles need rest — and if it persists, see a physio.
03

Pre-marathon preparation

The work is done in training. Race week is about arriving at the start line fresh, fuelled and calm. Here's how to handle the final stretch — and it applies to every distance, scaled up or down.

Taper & the final 3 weeks

3 weeks to race day
  • Cut volume, keep sharpness. Reduce weekly mileage by ~20–25% each week; keep a few short bursts of race-pace work so your legs stay quick.
  • Sleep is the taper. Aim for 7–9 hours. This is where adaptation actually happens.
  • Don't cram. Missed sessions are gone — adding them now only risks fatigue or injury.

Fuelling & carb-loading

72 hours to race day
  • Carb-load 2–3 days out for half-marathon and longer: increase carbohydrate intake while trimming fibre and heavy fats.
  • Practise your race fuel in training — gels, chews, or real food — never try anything new on race day.
  • Hydrate steadily, don't over-drink. Pale-yellow urine is the target; sip electrolytes in heat.

Hydration & electrolytes

Race week
  • Sodium matters for long efforts and hot days — use a sports drink or electrolyte tabs, not plain water alone.
  • Learn your sweat rate on long runs: weigh yourself before and after to estimate fluid loss per hour.
  • Don't chug litres the morning of the race — you'll be queuing at the portaloos, not running.

Day before

T-1 day
  • Short shakeout of 15–20 easy minutes with a few strides, then rest.
  • Lay out everything: kit, shoes, bib, timing chip, gels, anti-chafe, spare layer, bag drop.
  • Eat familiar food, early and not too heavy. Set two alarms. Sleep matters less than staying calm.

Race morning

T-0 hours
  • Wake 3 hours before the gun; eat a familiar, carb-focused breakfast.
  • Arrive early, use the loos, drop your bag, then warm up for 10–15 minutes with dynamic drills and strides.
  • Start easy. The first kilometres should feel almost too slow — the real race starts halfway.

Pacing & the mind

On the day
  • Even splits beat heroic starts. Go out 5–10 seconds per km slower than goal pace.
  • Break the race into segments — to the next aid station, the next kilometre — not the whole distance.
  • Have a mantra and a plan B. If it goes wrong, slow down, fuel, and finish strong.

Post-marathon recovery

Crossing the line is the halfway mark of the job. Recovery is training too — the reverse taper gets you back to running stronger, and skipping it is how niggles become injuries.

First 24–48 hours

Immediately after
  • Keep moving gently. A slow walk beats sitting still — it flushes the legs and helps you feel human.
  • Refuel within an hour: carbs plus protein, and rehydrate with electrolytes.
  • Compression and elevation can ease swelling. Skip the ice bath debate — do what feels good.

Week 1 — reverse taper

Days 2–7
  • No running for most people. Swap it for walking, easy cycling or swimming once soreness fades.
  • Eat well and sleep lots. Your immune system dips after a big effort — look after yourself.
  • Gentle mobility for calves, hips and quads, but don't force deep stretching into sore muscle.

Weeks 2–4 — return to running

Getting back
  • Start with easy, short runs — 20–30 minutes, every other day, only when you feel ready.
  • Build slowly. Return to normal mileage over 3–6 weeks; longer for a marathon or ultra.
  • Leave speed work out until you've had several pain-free, comfortable weeks.

Mobility & strength

Ongoing
  • Rebuild the basics: calf raises, glute bridges, single-leg balance and hip work.
  • Address what hurt. Post-race is the perfect time to fix the weakness behind a niggle.
  • Foam-roll gently, never aggressively over tender spots.

Nutrition & sleep

The repair crew
  • Protein at every meal to rebuild muscle; don't diet right after a big race.
  • Iron and vitamin D matter for runners — get levels checked if you feel flat for weeks.
  • Chase sleep. It's the single most powerful recovery tool you own.

Red flags — see a professional

When to stop
  • Pain that worsens with running, or that persists beyond a couple of weeks.
  • Sharp, localised or bone-deep pain — especially in the shin or foot — needs a physio or doctor.
  • Chest pain, breathlessness, dizziness or a racing heart at rest: seek medical help immediately.
Post-race blues are real. Many runners feel flat or low in the weeks after a big goal race. It's normal. Keep some gentle routine, set a new small goal, and talk to someone if it lingers — a doctor or a mental-health professional can help.
